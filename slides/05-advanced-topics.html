From Population to Sample

Where the uncertainty was hiding all week.

Why: Populations and Parameters

  • A population is all the values there are — every unit you care about, not a selection of them.
  • A parameter is one number that describes that whole population.
    • The mean age of every adult; the mean income of every household; the mean growth rate of every country.
  • Parameters are fixed — not random, they simply are — and unknown, because knowing one would mean measuring everybody.
  • So you take a sample, and on it you compute a statistic: the same kind of number, from the rows you actually have.

Greek and Hats: The Naming Convention

Population parameter Sample statistic
mean \(\mu\) \(\bar x\) (i.e. \(\hat\mu\))
standard deviation \(\sigma\) \(s\) (i.e. \(\hat\sigma\))
proportion \(\pi\) \(\hat p\)

Greek for what you want; a hat, or a Roman letter, for what you computed. The hat means estimated from data.

The Estimand: What You Actually Want to Know

  • A parameter is a fact about a population. An estimand is a fact about your question. These are not the same kind of thing.
  • The estimand is chosen before the data, by you, out of the research question. The parameter is just whatever the population happens to have.
  • Sometimes they coincide — and sometimes no parameter of your data is your estimand at all:
    • Question: mean growth of all countries. Estimand \(= \mu\), a parameter of the population you sampled. They coincide.
    • Question: the effect of democracy on growth.
      • Estimand \(= E[G(1)-G(0)]\) — about worlds that never happened. No mean of any dataset equals it.

Estimand, Estimator, Estimate

  • Used as a guess at the parameter, the recipe is an estimator; the number it returns on your data is an estimate.
  • Three words, three types. Keeping them apart is most of the discipline:
    • Estimand \(\theta\) — what you want. Fixed, unknown, chosen.
    • Estimator \(\hat\theta = g(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) — the recipe. A random variable, because the data are.
    • Estimate \(\hat\theta_{\text{obs}}\) — the number, 2.7. Not random; it already happened.
  • Two properties you will hear constantly, both about the middle one:
    • Unbiased: \(E[\hat\theta] = \theta\) — right on average across samples, not right in yours.
    • Consistent: \(\hat\theta \to \theta\) as \(n \to \infty\).
  • Keeping them apart is what makes a results sentence precise.
    • “The estimate is 2.7” reports this sample. “The effect is 2.7” is a claim about the estimand.
    • Getting from the first to the second takes assumptions, and they should be stated.

Intuition: Two Worlds

Solid arrow: you draw a sample. Dashed green arrow: the statistic is used to estimate the parameter. That green arrow is the whole subject.

When Is the Estimand Simply the Parameter?

  • The green arrow claims \(\bar x\) speaks for \(\mu\).
    • It speaks for what you wanted only under three conditions — all assumptions, none checkable from the data alone:
    • Representativeness — the population you sampled is the population your question is about.
    • Measurement — the thing you measured is the thing you meant. (Polyarchy is itself an estimate, with V-Dem’s own credible intervals.)
    • Descriptive, not causal — the question asks what is, not what would have been.
  • Break any one and \(\bar x\) still estimates \(\mu\) perfectly well. It just stops estimating your estimand.
  • Our data breaks the third one, badly. That is the last block of the afternoon.

Frequentist and Bayesian

You have been doing one of these all afternoon without being told.

Everything So Far Has Been Frequentist

  • Look back at what we just built. Every move rested on one commitment: the parameter is fixed; the data are random.
    • \(\mu\) was “fixed · unknown · never seen” — never random.
    • \(\hat\theta\) was the random variable, because the sample could have come out otherwise.
    • The three conditions were about whether \(\bar x\) could be trusted to speak for a \(\mu\) that sits still while we sample.
  • That commitment has a name.
    • The frequentist school holds the parameter of interest to be constant but unknown.
    • Probability is defined as long-run frequency over repeated trials.
  • Its characteristic sentence: there is a truth, a right answer, and I can be confident to some degree that I have found it.
  • Nothing forced this on us. It was smuggled in on the very first slide, when we called \(\mu\) fixed.

How Bayesians Differ

  • Bayesian: the parameter of interest is treated as a random variable.
    • Not because \(\mu\) varies — because probability is used to represent uncertainty about \(\mu\).
  • Probability therefore attaches to propositions — including “\(\mu\) lies in this interval” — and not only to repeatable events.
  • Both schools use the same machinery from Day 2. Write \(H\) for a hypothesis about the parameter and \(e\) for the evidence: \[p(H \mid e) \;=\; \frac{p(e \mid H)\,p(H)}{p(e)}\]
  • Nobody disputes this theorem. The schools differ over which term is the object of inference.

\(p(e \mid H)\) and \(p(H \mid e)\)

  • Frequentist inference works with \(p(e \mid H)\) — the probability of the evidence, with the hypothesis held fixed.
    • A \(p\)-value is \(p(\text{evidence this extreme} \mid H_0)\).
    • The coverage statement behind a confidence interval has the same shape: a probability about intervals, given a fixed \(\mu\).
  • Bayesian inference targets \(p(H \mid e)\) — the probability of the hypothesis, given the evidence in hand.
  • These are different quantities. Reporting one and reading it as the other is the most common misreading in applied work.

The Prior Is What Converts One Into the Other

  • The theorem states exactly what turns \(p(e \mid H)\) into \(p(H \mid e)\): the prior \(p(H)\).
    • Without \(p(H)\), \(\;p(e \mid H)\) does not become \(p(H \mid e)\). There is no way around this.
  • So the disagreement is not about the algebra but about whether \(p(H)\) is a defined object:
    • Frequentist: \(H\) is not random, so \(p(H)\) has no referent — and neither does \(p(H \mid e)\).
    • Bayesian: \(p(H)\) states what was held before the data, and the theorem updates it to \(p(H \mid e)\).

The Coin That Splits Them

  • I am about to flip a fair coin. What is the probability it lands heads?
    • Frequentist: 50%. Bayesian: 50%. No disagreement.
  • I have already flipped it, and not looked. What is the probability that this coin is heads?
    • Bayesian: still 50% — my information hasn’t changed, and probability describes my information.
    • Frequentist: it is no longer a matter of probability. The coin is what it is. It is heads or it isn’t.
  • Nothing about the coin changed between the two questions. What changed is whether there is anything left to be random.

Now put \(\mu\) where the coin is. The sample has been drawn; the number has been computed. Is there anything left to be random?

The Same Interval, Two Readings

Every estimate you report will come with an interval attached — call it \([\,L,\,U\,]\). Where that width comes from is next term’s business. Both schools accept the arithmetic, and disagree entirely about the sentence it licenses.

Frequentist — a confidence interval

  • \(\mu\) is fixed; the interval is the random thing.
  • “95% of intervals built this way cover \(\mu\).”
  • The 95% is a property of the procedure, across studies — not of the one interval you have.
  • You may not say \(P(\mu \in [\,L,\,U\,]) = 0.95\).

Bayesian — a credible interval

  • \(\mu\) is the random thing; the data are what you conditioned on.
  • “Given prior and data, \(P(\mu \in [\,L,\,U\,]) = 0.95\).”
  • Exactly the sentence you wanted to say, and are now licensed to.
  • The price: you must state a prior, and the answer depends on it.

Where It Actually Bites

  • With lots of data and a mild prior, the two agree numerically. Most of the time the fight is about interpretation, not digits.
  • It bites when data are thin, when the prior is informative, or when you need a probability statement about a hypothesis for a decision.
  • Neither camp is a moral position, and you are not required to pick one this week.
    • You are required to notice which one a paper’s language is assuming.
  • Neither one rescues you from the next block. A credible interval around a biased estimate is a precisely-quantified wrong answer.

Quick Drill: Whose Sentence Is It?

  • (a) “There is a 90% probability the effect is positive.”
  • (b) “We reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level.”
  • (c) “With a weakly informative prior, the posterior mean is 2.7.”
  • (d) “Under repeated sampling this estimator is unbiased.”
  • Answers: (a) Bayesian. (b) frequentist. (c) Bayesian. (d) frequentist.
  • Now the harder one: which of these did you assume the regression output was telling you, before this afternoon?

In Practice: What Each One Costs

  • Frequentist: the estimate is usually a closed form or a quick optimization.
    • In R it is mostly built in — lm(), glm(), t.test(), confint() — and returns immediately.
  • Bayesian: the posterior \(p(H \mid e)\) rarely has a closed form.
    • It is sampled rather than solved, by MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo) — a long chain of draws whose histogram approximates the posterior.
    • Computationally heavy — seconds to hours — and the chains must be checked for convergence before the answer means anything.
  • Packages:
    • Frequentist — base stats, plus sandwich and fixest.
    • Bayesian — brms and rstanarm (both take lm()-style formulas, Stan underneath), MCMCpack, rjags.
  • To read:
    • Gelman & Hill, Data Analysis Using Regression and Multilevel/Hierarchical Models.
    • McElreath, Statistical Rethinking; Gelman et al., Bayesian Data Analysis.

Causality

Our unanswered question: does democracy cause growth?

The Estimate We Have

  • Let \(G\) be a country’s growth rate, and let \(D\) record its regime: \(D=1\) for a democracy, \(D=0\) for an autocracy.
  • Two quantities we already know how to compute:
    • \(E[G \mid D=1]\) — average growth among the democracies. Take the democracies, average their growth.
    • \(E[G \mid D=0]\) — average growth among the autocracies. Same move, other group.
  • Their difference is the number every paper on this question reports: \[E[G \mid D=1] - E[G \mid D=0]\]
  • What it gives us: an honest description. Democracies grew faster, or slower, than autocracies — by this much.
    • Nothing more is needed to compute it. It is two group averages and a subtraction.

(Your courses will write \(Y\) where we are writing \(G\). Same object.)

Why That Is Not Yet a Cause

  • A cause would mean: these countries grew faster because they were democracies. The comparison above cannot say that, for two reasons.
  • The two groups were already different.
    • Richer, older states with settled institutions sit on one side; poorer states catching up sit on the other. They would not have grown alike even under identical regimes.
  • Regimes are not handed out at random.
    • Countries that became democracies were the kind of country that becomes a democracy — and whatever made that happen may drive growth too.
  • Neither problem shrinks with more countries. Both are about which countries are in which group, not how many.
  • To see why, it helps to imagine a table we can never actually have.

If We Could See Both Columns

Imagine we could observe each country twice — once as a democracy, once not.

Country Democracy? D Growth if democratic Growth if not Difference
Norway Yes 1 2.1 1.4 +0.7
Denmark Yes 1 1.8 1.5 +0.3
Saudi Arabia No 0 5.6 6.4 −0.8
North Korea No 0 0.9 0.6 +0.3

The last column is, for each country, the effect of democracy on its own growth — what democracy did for that country. Average it and you have the effect of democracy, full stop: \((0.7 + 0.3 - 0.8 + 0.3)/4 = +0.125\).

Illustrative values, not data.

In the Real World, One Column Is Blank

A country is either a democracy or it isn’t. We never see the other version of it.

Country Democracy? D Growth if democratic Growth if not Difference
Norway Yes 1 2.1 ? ?
Denmark Yes 1 1.8 ? ?
Saudi Arabia No 0 ? 6.4 ?
North Korea No 0 ? 0.6 ?
  • Every ? is a number that was never recorded anywhere. Norway’s growth without democratisation isn’t missing from the dataset — it never happened.
  • So not one row of the difference column can be filled in. Not for any country, ever.
  • All we can do is compare the two visible columns: \(\;\text{mean}(2.1,\,1.8) - \text{mean}(6.4,\,0.6) = -1.55\).
    • The true answer, one slide ago, was \(+0.125\). The visible comparison gets the sign wrong.

Now the Notation

The two columns you just saw have names, and you will meet them constantly.

What we called it Notation Read it as
Growth if democratic \(G_i(1)\) country \(i\)’s growth in the world where it is a democracy
Growth if not \(G_i(0)\) the same country’s growth in the world where it is not
Difference \(G_i(1) - G_i(0)\) the causal effect of democracy for country \(i\)
Average of that column \(\tau = E[G(1) - G(0)]\) the average causal effect — our estimand

\(\tau\) (“tau”) is just a name. It is the Greek letter conventionally used for a causal effect, exactly as \(\mu\) was the letter for a mean — and like \(\mu\) it is a parameter: one fixed, unknown number we are trying to learn.

The Fundamental Problem

  • \(G_i(1)\) and \(G_i(0)\) are the potential outcomes. Both exist; only one is ever observed.
  • That is the fundamental problem of causal inference, and it is a missing-data problem.
    • More countries add more rows. Every new row still arrives with one side blank.
  • Which is why \(\tau\) is an estimand and not a statistic: no dataset contains the column it averages.

\(Y\) and \(X\): The General Notation

Democracy and growth were one instance. The general vocabulary:

  • \(X\) — the treatment, the thing whose effect you want.
    • Often binary (\(X=1\) treated, \(X=0\) untreated): democracy, a job programme, a get-out-the-vote call.
    • It can also be continuous — years of schooling, dose, a polyarchy score — in which case “the effect” means the effect of changing \(X\).
  • \(Y\) — the outcome, the thing you measure afterwards.
  • \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) — the potential outcomes: unit \(i\)’s outcome if treated, and if not.
    • The word potential is doing real work. Both are properties of the unit, whichever one the world happens to reveal.
  • \(W\) — covariates, everything else you know about the unit before treatment.

From here on: \(Y\) and \(X\), not \(G\) and \(D\). Same objects, the notation your courses use.

What We Compute vs. What We Want

blue = we observe it · red = it never happened, so we never see it

What we compute: \(E[Y \mid X{=}1]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y \mid X{=}0]\)

The same thing, in potential outcomes: \(E[Y(1) \mid X{=}1]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}0]\)

What we want: \(E[Y(1) \mid X{=}1]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}1]\)

the same countries, treated and untreated

The two lines differ in one term only — and it is the red one.

The Bias Is That One Red Term

\(\text{bias} \;=\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}1]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}0]\)

  • We wanted to subtract the red term. We subtracted the blue one instead, because it is the one that exists in the data.
  • So the number we compute is the effect plus this gap, and the gap has a name: baseline bias.
  • Read it in words: how the two groups would have compared had neither been treated.
    • Norway and North Korea do not share a \(Y(0)\). Democracy is not what makes them differ.
  • If the treated group would have done better anyway, the gap is positive and our number overstates the effect.
  • It contains a red term, so it can never be computed. No larger \(n\) turns it blue.

Selection Bias

  • Selection is the mechanism behind that gap: units are not assigned treatment, they end up with it.
  • Two ways it bites:
    • Selection on levels — units that would have done better anyway are likelier to be treated. This is the baseline bias above.
    • Selection on gains — units that would benefit most are likelier to be treated. Then the effect on the treated differs from the effect on everyone else.
  • Many texts use “selection bias” for the baseline term itself. Notice which sense is meant.
  • Both are about which units are in which group — never about how many.

ATE — Average Treatment Effect

\(\tau_{\text{ATE}} \;=\; E\big[\,Y(1) - Y(0)\,\big]\)

  • The average effect over everybody, treated and untreated alike.
  • If we treated the whole population instead of nobody, how far would the average outcome move?
  • It is the default estimand — and frequently not the one a study can deliver.

ATT — Effect on the Treated

\(\tau_{\text{ATT}} \;=\;\) \(E[Y(1) \mid X{=}1]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}1]\)

  • The average effect among the units that were actually treated.
  • For the countries that did democratise, what did democratising do for them?
  • Notice the colours: one half is observed. That is why observational work so often targets the ATT — you are missing one term, not two.

ATC — Effect on the Controls

\(\tau_{\text{ATC}} \;=\;\) \(E[Y(1) \mid X{=}0]\) \(\;-\;\) \(E[Y(0) \mid X{=}0]\)

  • The average effect among the units that were not treated — what treatment would have done to them.
  • What would democratising the remaining autocracies do? The policy question, and the harder one.
  • The two combine into the ATE, weighted by the share treated \(\pi\):
    • \(\tau_{\text{ATE}} = \pi\,\tau_{\text{ATT}} + (1-\pi)\,\tau_{\text{ATC}}\)
  • When \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} \neq \tau_{\text{ATC}}\) the effect is heterogeneous — treatment does different things to different units.

CATE — Conditional Average Treatment Effect

\(\tau_{\text{CATE}}(w) \;=\; E\big[\,Y(1) - Y(0) \,\big|\, W = w\,\big]\)

  • The average effect within a subgroup picked out by covariates \(W\) — by region, income level, prior institutions.
  • It is not one number but a function of \(w\): a different effect for each kind of unit.
  • This is where “does democracy cause growth?” ends up once people take it seriously.
    • Not one effect for all countries, but an effect that depends on what the country already is.
  • Four estimands — ATE, ATT, ATC, CATE — from one table. Choose before you estimate.

Why Some Designs Escape the Problem

  • Both bias terms come from one thing: who ends up treated is related to what would have happened to them.
  • A design escapes when treatment is assigned exogenously — from outside the system, by something unrelated to the potential outcomes.
    • Exogenous = determined outside the process you are studying. Endogenous = determined within it.
    • A country’s regime is endogenous to its history, wealth and institutions. A coin flip is not.
  • Written down: assignment independent of the potential outcomes, \(\;\big(Y(1), Y(0)\big) \perp X\).
    • Then \(E[Y(0)\mid X{=}1] = E[Y(0)\mid X{=}0]\), so baseline bias is zero; and \(\tau_{\text{ATT}} = \tau_{\text{ATC}}\), so the third term is zero too.
  • The left side of the decomposition then equals the ATE. The statistic becomes the estimand.
  • Everything that follows is a catalogue of ways to get partway there when you cannot randomise.

Randomised Experiments

  • What it does: a coin, not the unit, decides who is treated.
  • Why it works: assignment is independent of \(Y(1)\) and \(Y(0)\) by construction, so both bias terms are zero — not assumed away, built away.

Difference-in-Differences

  • What it does: compares the change over time in a treated group against the change in an untreated one.
  • Why it works: fixed baseline differences cancel in the subtraction — provided the two groups would have moved in parallel absent treatment.

Regression Discontinuity

  • What it does: compares units just above and just below a threshold that mechanically determines treatment.
  • Why it works: near the cutoff, which side you land on is as good as random — so the jump at the line is the effect, for units near the line.

Instrumental Variables

  • What it does: finds a variable \(Z\) that shifts treatment \(X\) but touches the outcome only through \(X\).
  • Why it works: the part of \(X\) that \(Z\) moves is unrelated to the confounder \(U\) — so that part, and only that part, gives a clean comparison.

Matching

  • What it does: pairs each treated unit with untreated units that look the same on observed covariates \(W\), then compares within pairs.
  • Why it works — and when it doesn’t: it removes bias from what you measured. If anything unobserved drives selection, the bias survives, and no diagnostic will tell you.

The Dialect Equivalence

  • Your econometrics course will state the key assumption as exogeneity: \(\;E[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\).
  • Your causal inference course will state it as the selection-bias term is zero.
  • These are the same claim in two notations. Different courses, different textbooks, different vocabulary, one idea.
  • In English, for our data: whatever else drives growth is unrelated to how democratic a country is.
    • Say it out loud about the V-Dem cross-section and hear how implausible it is.
  • Recognizing this equivalence when you meet it in October is the single most useful thing this week can give you.

Quick Drill: Which Gap?

  • Three gaps stand between us and Monday’s question. Which one does each fix close?
    • (a) Collect 30 more countries.
    • (b) Randomly assign democracy to countries.
    • (c) Use V-Dem’s credible intervals instead of the point estimate for polyarchy.
    • (d) Add region and prior-income controls.
  • Answers:
      1. estimation only — narrower interval, same bias. (b) identification — and is unavailable, which is the point.
      1. measurement. (d) maybe identification, conditional on an untestable assumption you now know how to state.
  • Only estimation closes with \(n\). The other two don’t, ever.
