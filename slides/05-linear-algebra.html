Vectors

A column of numbers you have already been staring at all week.

Why We Need Them

  • Our dataset is \(n \approx 175\) countries, each with a polyarchy score \(x_i\) and a growth rate \(y_i\).
  • Everything we did on Days 1–4 was written one country at a time and then summed: \[\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\]
  • That \(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\) is doing a lot of work, and it is doing the same work every time.
    • A vector is what lets us stop writing it.

Intuition: The Data Column

  • Careful with the words. The whole table is a matrix — rows and columns together. A single column of it is a vector.
    • So a data frame isn’t a vector; it’s a collection of them, stacked side by side. We’ll come back to the whole table later this hour.
  • A vector is an ordered list of numbers — unlike a set, the order is part of the object, and a repeated value is not the same as one value.
  • Order matters here for a concrete reason: row 2 is India in \(x\) and in \(y\). Shuffling one column breaks the pairing between them.

Formal: Columns, Rows, and the Prime

  • A column vector with \(n\) entries is an \(n \times 1\) object; a row vector is \(1 \times n\). \[\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}_{n \times 1}, \qquad \mathbf{x}' = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix}_{1 \times n}\]
  • Convention (near-universal in stats): unadorned means column. The prime \('\) (or superscript \(\top\)) turns it on its side.
    • Same \(n\) numbers, same order, different shape — and the shape, as the next slide shows, is what decides which operations you can write down at all.
  • In R this is just a numeric column: df$polyarchy is \(\mathbf{x}\).

Every Object Has a Shape

Before any operation at all, the rule that governs every one of them.

  • Everything today is a rectangle of numbers, described by rows × columns — rows first, always.
    • A single number is \(1\times1\). A column vector is \(n\times1\). A row vector is \(1\times n\). A matrix is \(m\times n\).
    • Four names, one family. The shape is the only thing that changes.
  • Shapes decide what you can write down. Two rules, and that’s the whole of it:
    • Conformable for addition: the shapes must be identical. \((n\times1) + (n\times1)\) ✓, \((n\times1) + (1\times n)\) ✗.
    • Conformable for multiplication: the inner dimensions must match. They cancel; the outer pair survives as the answer’s shape.
  • Conformable is the word — it’s what you’ll see in print, so use it rather than “allowed” or “works”. When shapes aren’t conformable the operation simply does not exist: the expression isn’t wrong, it’s meaningless, like dividing by a colour.

Conformable or Not: Reading the Shapes

  • Read every matrix line this way and most of your first year becomes checkable: name the shapes, see if the inner pair matches.
  • We cash this in twice today: a row times a column gives \((1\times n)(n\times 1) = 1\times1\) — a single number; a matrix times a column gives \((n\times k)(k\times 1) = n\times1\) — a whole column.
    • Both are the same rule. Neither needs new arithmetic.

Formal: Adding and Scaling

Two operations on vectors. Both are done entry by entry, and both obey the rules from the last slide.

Addition — add matching entries. Conformable only when the shapes are identical.

\[\begin{bmatrix} 0.91 \\ 0.56 \\ 0.28 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.03 \\ 0.10 \\ 0.02 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.94 \\ 0.66 \\ 0.30 \end{bmatrix}\]

\((n\times1) + (n\times1) = (n\times1)\)

Scalar multiplication — multiply every entry by one number. Always conformable; shape never changes.

\[2\begin{bmatrix} 0.91 \\ 0.56 \\ 0.28 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.82 \\ 1.12 \\ 0.56 \end{bmatrix}\]

\((1\times1)\cdot(n\times1) = (n\times1)\)

One vector is worth naming: \(\mathbf{1}\), the \(n\times1\) column of all ones. Scale it and you get a column of identical numbers — \(\bar x\,\mathbf{1}\) is \(\bar x\) repeated \(n\) times. That is the standard move for turning a lone number into something you’re allowed to add to or subtract from a column.

Example: Centering Is a Vector Operation

One country at a time: \(\tilde x_i = x_i - \bar x\). As vectors, using the \(\mathbf{1}\) from the last slide: \(\;\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \bar{x}\,\mathbf{1}\).

The \(\mathbf{1}\) is there to make the shapes match — you can’t subtract a lone number from a column, so you stretch it into one.

What Centering Did, and Didn’t

  • Didn’t change: the spacing between countries. Norway is still \(0.77\) above Chad, before and after.
  • Did change: where zero sits. Zero now means average, so the sign of an entry tells you which side of the mean a country is on.
  • Across all \(n\), the centered column sums to zero: \(\sum_i(x_i - \bar x) = 0\).
  • The point of the notation: one line replaced \(n\) lines, and nothing conceptual changed.
    • In R you’ve been doing it this way since Monday: x - mean(x).

Quick Drill: Vectors by the Numbers

Let \(\;\mathbf{a} = (2,\;5,\;2)'\;\) and \(\;\mathbf{b} = (4,\;0,\;3)'\;\) — both \(3\times1\) — and let \(\;\mathbf{1} = (1,\;1,\;1)'\).

  • (a) \(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \;=\;\) \((6,\;5,\;5)'\) — add matching entries. Still \(3\times1\).
  • (b) \(2\,\mathbf{a} \;=\;\) \((4,\;10,\;4)'\) — every entry doubled. Shape unchanged.
  • (c) \(\bar a \;=\;\) \(3\), so \(\;\tilde{\mathbf{a}} = \mathbf{a} - 3\,\mathbf{1} = (-1,\;2,\;-1)'\) — and it sums to \(0\), as a centered column always must.
  • (d) What shape is \(\mathbf{a}'\), and are \(\;\mathbf{a}\;\) and \(\;\mathbf{b}'\;\) conformable for addition? \(\mathbf{a}'\) is \(1\times3\). Not conformable — \(3\times1\) and \(1\times3\) are different shapes, even with the same three numbers.
  • (e) R evaluates a + 5 without complaint. What comes back, and what should you have written? \((7,\;10,\;7)'\) — R silently recycled the \(5\). Properly: \(\;\mathbf{a} + 5\,\mathbf{1}\).

(e) is the one to remember. Software’s willingness to guess is exactly why you need the rule in your own head.

The Dot Product

The one operation that is secretly every formula you learned this week.

Why We Need It

  • Three sums keep turning up wherever two columns of data meet:
    • \(\sum_i x_i\) — the numerator of the mean.
    • \(\sum_i x_i^2\) — a sum of squares.
    • \(\sum_i x_i y_i\) — the cross-product of two variables.
  • Every one of them is the same shape of operation: multiply in pairs, then add up.
  • Give that operation a name once, and all three collapse into one piece of notation.

Intuition: A Row Times a Column

  • Each colour is one pair: the first entry of the row with the first entry of the column, and so on down the line.
  • Multiply within each pair, then add the four results. Two vectors in, one number out.
  • \((1\times 4)\) times \((4\times 1)\) gives \((1\times 1)\): the two \(4\)s faced each other and cancelled, the two \(1\)s survived.

Formal: The Dot Product

  • For \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\) both \(n\times1\): \[\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i \qquad (1\times n)(n\times1) = 1\times 1\]
  • Note the shapes multiply the way you’d hope: the inner dimensions (\(n\) and \(n\)) meet and vanish; the outer dimensions (\(1\) and \(1\)) survive.
    • That is the whole rule of matrix multiplication. We will state it again in a moment for the general case, but you have now seen it.
  • Special case worth memorizing: \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{x} = \sum_i x_i^2\) — a sum of squares, always \(\ge 0\).

One Operation, Three Names

Textbooks call this same thing three different things. They are synonyms — the difference is only which feature the name points at.

  • Dot product — named for the notation, a centred dot. The elementary name.
    • \(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i\)
  • Scalar product — named for what comes out: a single scalar.
    • \(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} \;=\; 4.648\), a \(1\times1\)
  • Inner product — the general name, and the one econometrics tends to use.
    • \(\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}\rangle\) in abstract notation; \(\;\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\) in matrix notation

In this deck: “dot product”, written \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\). When you meet the other two names, they are not new operations.

Two Traps in Those Names

  • Scalar product \(\neq\) scalar multiplication. One word apart, completely different operations.
    • Scalar product: \(\;\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\) — two vectors in, one number out. \((1\times n)(n\times1) = 1\times1\).
    • Scalar multiplication: \(\;c\,\mathbf{x}\) — a number and a vector in, a vector out, same shape as \(\mathbf{x}\).
  • Inner product \(\neq\) outer product. Same two vectors, opposite order, wildly different results.
    • Inner: \(\;\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\) — \((1\times n)(n\times1) = 1\times1\), a number.
    • Outer: \(\;\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}'\) — \((n\times1)(1\times n) = n\times n\), a whole matrix.

Both traps are settled the same way: name the shapes. One prime in the wrong place is the difference between a number and \(n^2\) of them.

Example: Familiar Formulas, Compressed

Write \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \bar x\mathbf{1}\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y} - \bar y\mathbf{1}\) for the centered columns, as on the last slide.

  • Sum: \(\;\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{x} = \sum_i x_i\), so the mean is \(\bar x = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}'\mathbf{x}\).
  • Sum of squares: \(\;\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{x} = \sum_i x_i^2\).
  • The variance’s numerator: \(\;\tilde{\mathbf{x}}'\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\) — how much polyarchy moves on its own.
  • The covariance’s numerator: \(\;\tilde{\mathbf{x}}'\tilde{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)\) — how polyarchy and growth move together.
  • The same arithmetic you would write with \(\sum\), in fewer symbols.
    • Keep these last two in view. When we read \(X'X\) and \(X'y\) at the end of the hour, they are exactly these two objects with more columns.

Quick Drill: Is It a Number or a Vector?

  • \(\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) are both \(n\times 1\). For each, give the shape of the result:
    • (a) \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\)
    • (b) \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}'\)
    • (c) \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}\)
  • Answers: (a) \(1\times1\) — a single number, the dot product. (b) \(n \times n\) — a whole matrix, the outer product. (c) not conformable: \((n\times1)(n\times1)\), inner dimensions \(1\) and \(n\) don’t match.
  • Moral: \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}'\) differ by one prime and by \(n^2 - 1\) numbers.

Matrices

A stack of vectors, with dimensions you must keep in view.

Why We Need Them

  • So far: one predictor, polyarchy. But no one believes growth depends on democracy alone.
  • Add GDP per capita in 2000, region dummies, an oil indicator, a colonial-origin indicator…
  • Written out one predictor at a time, a \(k\)-predictor model needs \(k\) separate equations, each with its own nested summations.
    • Doable. Unreadable — and it gets worse with every variable you add.
  • Matrices are how the same model is written so it fits on one line, for any \(k\).

Intuition: The Design Matrix

  • A matrix is your data frame with the country names peeled off and only the numbers left.
  • The leading column of \(1\)s is not data — it’s the bookkeeping device that gives you an intercept. We’ll see exactly why when we read the model row by row at the end.

Formal: Dimensions and Elements

  • \(A\) is \(m \times n\): \(m\) rows, \(n\) columns. Rows first, always — the same convention as vectors, where a column was \(n\times1\).
  • A single entry is \(a_{ij}\): row \(i\), column \(j\). Row index first, again. \[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{2\times 3} \qquad a_{11} = 1, \quad a_{23} = 6, \quad a_{12} = 2\]
  • \(a_{23}\) is row 2, column 3 — not “the 23rd entry”, and not column 2, row 3.
    • Getting this backwards is the most common slip when reading a summation like \(\sum_i x_{ij}\).
  • For our data, \(X\) is \(n\times k\): \(x_{ij}\) is country \(i\)’s value on variable \(j\).

Formal: Multiplication and Conformability

  • The rule from the Vectors section, now in general. \(AB\) exists only if the columns of \(A\) equal the rows of \(B\): \[\underset{m\times \color{#2b6cb0}{n}}{A}\;\underset{\color{#2b6cb0}{n}\times p}{B} \;=\; \underset{m \times p}{C}, \qquad c_{ij} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{n} a_{i\ell}\,b_{\ell j}\]
  • Entry \(c_{ij}\) is exactly the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\). Nothing new since two sections ago.
  • Inner dimensions must match and disappear; outer dimensions survive.
  • \(AB \ne BA\). Order is meaning, not style. Often \(BA\) isn’t even conformable.

Formal: The Transpose

The same prime you met on vectors, now for a rectangle: rows become columns.

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{2\times3} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A' = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{3\times2}\]

  • Row 1 of \(A\) becomes column 1 of \(A'\); row 2 becomes column 2.
  • Formally \((A')_{ij} = a_{ji}\) — the indices swap, and an \(m\times n\) becomes \(n\times m\).

Two Transpose Rules, and What They Build

  • \((A')' = A\) — do it twice and you’re back where you started.
  • \((AB)' = B'A'\) — the order reverses. Not a typo, and the step most often got wrong when reading a variance derivation.

Now we can build the object this hour keeps pointing at. \(X\) is \(n\times k\), so \(X'\) is \(k\times n\):

\[X'X \;=\; (k\times n)(n\times k) \;=\; k\times k\]

Small and square however many countries there are — the \(n\) met itself in the middle and cancelled.

Named by Shape: Scalars and Columns

The first set of names say nothing but how many rows and columns.

Scalar — \(1\times1\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}7\end{bmatrix}\]

An ordinary number, wearing brackets. A mean, a variance, or the \(\mathbf{x}'\mathbf{y}\) you computed a moment ago.

Column vector — \(n\times1\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}1.2\\4.9\\2.1\end{bmatrix}\]

One variable, one entry per country. The outcome \(\mathbf{y}\) is one of these.

Named by Shape: Rows and Rectangles

Row vector — \(1\times n\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}1.2 & 4.9 & 2.1\end{bmatrix}\]

One country, one entry per variable. A single row of the data table.

Rectangular — \(m \neq n\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{bmatrix}\]

More rows than columns, or fewer. The design matrix \(X\) is one: \(n \times k\), and \(n\) is far bigger.

Named by Shape: Square

Square — \(m = n\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\]

As many rows as columns.

We built one two slides ago:

\[X'X \;=\; (k\times n)(n\times k) \;=\; k\times k\]

Square whatever \(n\) is: \(175\) countries or \(17\), \(X'X\) is still \(k\times k\).

One family, four names so far. None of these is a new kind of object — a scalar is simply a matrix that ran out of rows and columns.

Named by Structure: Symmetric and Diagonal

The second set say where the zeros are, or how a matrix relates to its own transpose. Both of these are square.

Symmetric — \(A = A'\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}4&\color{#2b6cb0}{1}\\ \color{#2b6cb0}{1}&3\end{bmatrix}\]

Swap rows for columns and nothing changes. Every variance–covariance matrix is one.

Diagonal — off-diagonal entries all \(0\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\]

Only the top-left-to-bottom-right entries survive. It scales each row by its own number and does nothing else.

Named by Structure: The Identity

Identity \(I\) — \(1\)s down the middle

\[I = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\]

\[IA = AI = A\]

The matrix world’s number \(1\): multiply by it and nothing changes.

The nesting. Every identity is diagonal; every diagonal is square; and symmetric implies square too, since \(A\) and \(A'\) can’t be equal unless the shape survives the swap.

And \(X'X\) is symmetric — using only the two transpose rules: \[(X'X)' = X'(X')' = X'X \;\checkmark\]

Example: Reading Shapes Off the Regression

A regression model, written for \(k\) predictors at once. Our only job right now is to check the shapes.

  • \(X\) is \(n\times k\), \(\beta\) is \(k\times1\), \(y\) is \(n\times1\), \(\varepsilon\) is \(n\times1\).
  • \[\underset{n\times1}{y} \;=\; \underset{n\times k}{X}\,\underset{k\times1}{\beta} \;+\; \underset{n\times1}{\varepsilon}\]
  • Check it: \((n\times k)(k\times1) = n\times1\). ✓ Matches \(y\). ✓ Matches \(\varepsilon\). The shapes conform.
  • Row \(i\) of \(X\beta\) spells out \(\;\beta_1 + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \beta_3 x_{i3}\) — the leading column of \(1\)s is what makes \(\beta_1\) the intercept.

Quick Drill: Types and Transposes

  • (a) \(A\) is \(3\times5\). What shape is \(A'\)? \(5\times3\) — the dimensions swap.
  • (b) Can a \(3\times5\) matrix be symmetric? No. \(A'\) would be \(5\times3\), so \(A = A'\) can’t even be written down. Symmetric requires square.
  • (c) \(B = \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&7\end{bmatrix}\). Name every label that fits. Square, diagonal, and symmetric — a diagonal matrix always equals its own transpose. Not the identity, since the entries aren’t \(1\)s.
  • (d) \(X\) is \(n\times k\). Is \(X'X\) symmetric? Yes — \((X'X)' = X'(X')' = X'X\), using \((AB)'=B'A'\) and \((A')'=A\). This is why variance–covariance matrices are always symmetric.
  • (e) What is \(IA\), for \(I\) the identity of the right size? \(A\) itself — multiplying by \(I\) changes nothing, exactly as multiplying a number by \(1\) changes nothing.

Quick Drill: Conformability

  • \(X\) is \(n\times k\), \(y\) is \(n\times 1\). Conformable or not — and if so, what shape?
    • (a) \(X'X\)
    • (b) \(XX'\)
    • (c) \(X'y\)
    • (d) \(Xy\)
  • Answers: (a) \(k\times k\) ✓. (b) \(n\times n\) ✓ — conformable but enormous (\(175\times175\) here), and almost never what you want. (c) \(k\times1\) ✓. (d) not conformable: \((n\times k)(n\times1)\), inner dimensions \(k\) and \(n\) don’t match.
  • If you can do this drill reliably, you can audit most matrix lines you’ll be shown this year.

Regression as a System of Equations

What the matrix notation has been compressing all along.

Two Equations, Two Unknowns

Ordinary algebra, nothing new. Two facts about two unknown numbers:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x + y &= 5 \\ x - y &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

  • Add the two equations: the \(y\)s cancel, leaving \(3x = 6\), so \(x = 2\).
  • Substitute back: \(2 - y = 1\), so \(y = 1\).
  • Two equations, two unknowns, one exact answer. Hold on to that phrase — in a moment it stops being true.

The Same System, in Matrix Form

Pull the system apart into three pieces: the coefficients, the unknowns, and the right-hand sides.

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \]

  • Multiply out row 1: \(2x + 1y = 5\). Row 2: \(1x - 1y = 1\). The original system, exactly.
  • Shapes: \((2\times2)(2\times1) = 2\times1\) ✓ — conformable, matching \(\mathbf{b}\).
  • So matrix notation isn’t a new kind of mathematics. It’s a system of equations with the bookkeeping pulled out into its own boxes.

Undoing \(A\)

  • With one equation, \(ax = b\), you solve by dividing: \(x = b/a\).
  • With a matrix there is no division. Instead we write the undo step with a superscript \(-1\): \[A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}\]
  • \(A^{-1}\) is called the inverse of \(A\) — the matrix that undoes whatever \(A\) did.

The Inverse, on Our \(2\times2\)

\(A^{-1}\) is defined by what it does: \(\;A^{-1}A = I\), the identity from the zoo. For the system we just solved,

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\]

Check it — multiply them and the identity falls out:

\[A^{-1}A = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I \;\checkmark\]

And it solves the system: \(\;\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\) — the same \(x=2\), \(y=1\) we got by elimination.

The Model, Written Out

\[\underset{n\times1}{\mathbf{y}} \;=\; \underset{n\times k}{X}\;\underset{k\times1}{\boldsymbol\beta} \;+\; \underset{n\times1}{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}\]

Four countries, two predictors — so \(n = 4\) and \(k = 3\), because the intercept column counts:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1.2 \\ 4.9 \\ 2.1 \\ 1.6 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}\;(4\times1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.91 & 10.9 \\ 1 & 0.56 & 7.3 \\ 1 & 0.28 & 8.1 \\ 1 & 0.14 & 6.5 \end{bmatrix}}_{X\;(4\times3)} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \beta_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\beta\;(3\times1)} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon_1 \\ \varepsilon_2 \\ \varepsilon_3 \\ \varepsilon_4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol\varepsilon\;(4\times1)} \]

Shapes: \((4\times1) = (4\times3)(3\times1) + (4\times1)\) ✓ — conformable, and both sides come out \(4\times1\).

One Row at a Time

Same trick as the \(2\times2\): multiply out one row and you get back an ordinary equation. This is equation 2 of 4 — India:

\[ \underbrace{4.9}_{y_2} \;=\; \beta_1(1) \;+\; \beta_2(0.56) \;+\; \beta_3(7.3) \;+\; \varepsilon_2 \]

  • The leading \(1\) is what makes \(\beta_1\) an intercept — it multiplies the same \(1\) in every row, so it shifts every prediction equally.
  • \(\beta_2\) is what one full unit of polyarchy is worth in growth; \(\beta_3\) the same for log GDP per capita.
  • Four countries, four equations like this one, stacked. Nothing is hidden in the notation — it’s a system, written compactly.

Every Piece, Named

Object Shape What it holds Known?
\(\mathbf{y}\) \(4\times1\) growth, one entry per country known — it’s data
\(X\) \(4\times3\) a column of \(1\)s, polyarchy, log GDP per capita known — it’s data
\(\boldsymbol\beta\) \(3\times1\) one slope per column of \(X\) unknown — what we want
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) \(4\times1\) what the model misses, per country unknown

Two known, two unknown — the same situation as \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\), where \(A\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) were given and \(\mathbf{x}\) was not.

Taller Than It Is Wide

  • The \(2\times2\) system had two equations and two unknowns, and one exact answer.
  • The real dataset has \(n \approx 175\) equations and \(k = 3\) unknowns — one equation per country, three coefficients.
  • With that many more equations than unknowns, there is no exact answer. No three numbers satisfy all 175 equations at once.
    • Which is just the familiar fact that no straight line passes through every point of a scatterplot.
  • So we don’t solve the system. We take the best compromise — the \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) that misses by as little as possible, measured by squared misses.
    • That is what the name least squares refers to.

The Estimator, Read Left to Right

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \;=\; (X'X)^{-1}X'\mathbf{y}\]

Same four countries, so \(X\) is \(4\times3\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) is \(4\times1\):

  • \(X'\) — \((3\times4)\). The design matrix on its side.
  • \(X'\mathbf{y}\) — \((3\times4)(4\times1) = 3\times1\). One dot product per predictor with the outcome: the covariance-ish part.
  • \(X'X\) — \((3\times4)(4\times3) = 3\times3\). Square and symmetric: the variance-ish part. It stays \(3\times3\) — it never grows with the number of countries.
  • \((X'X)^{-1}\) — still \(3\times3\), and by now you know how to read it: undo the \(X'X\) part.
  • All together: \((3\times3)(3\times1) = 3\times1\). One number per column of \(X\). ✓

What Comes Out

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat\beta_1 \\ \hat\beta_2 \\ \hat\beta_3 \end{bmatrix}_{3\times1} \qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad \texttt{lm(growth ~ polyarchy + loggdp)}\]

  • Three columns in, three coefficients out — one per column of \(X\), in the order the columns appear.
  • That is exactly the coefficient table R prints: the matrix formula and the R output are the same object.

Quick Drill: Audit the Line

  • \(X\) is \(n\times k\) and \(\mathbf{y}\) is \(n\times1\). One of these is written correctly — which, and what’s wrong with the others?
    • (a) \(\hat\beta = (XX')^{-1}X'y\)
    • (b) \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}Xy\)
    • (c) \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\)
    • (d) \(\hat\beta = X'y(X'X)^{-1}\)
  • Answer: (c). Every wrong one fails on shape alone:
      1. \(XX'\) is \(n\times n\), and \(X'y\) is \(k\times1\) — so \((n\times n)(k\times1)\) is not conformable.
      1. \(Xy\) is not conformable: \((n\times k)(n\times1)\).
      1. \((k\times1)(k\times k)\) — inner dimensions don’t match. Order is meaning.
