Counting

Combinatorics: shortcuts for counting outcomes without listing every one.

Counting: Why We Need It

  • Pr(e) was a ratio of counts — but counting outcomes by hand stops working once the sample space gets big.
  • Combinatorics gives us shortcuts for counting without listing every possibility.
  • Two questions decide which shortcut applies: does order matter, and are we sampling with or without replacement?

The Fundamental Rule of Counting

  • If a choice is made in stages, and stage \(i\) has \(n_i\) independent options, the total number of ways to complete all stages is \(\prod_i n_i\) — multiply, don’t add.
  • Example: classifying one country by region (5 options) and by regime type (2 options) gives \(5 \times 2 = 10\) possible classifications.
  • This single multiplication rule is what everything else on this slide builds on.

Review of Factorials

  • \(n! = n \times (n-1) \times \cdots \times 1\) — the number of ways to arrange all \(n\) objects in order.
  • Example: \(3! = 3\times2\times1=6\). For countries \(\{X,Y,Z\}\), all 6 orderings: XYZ, XZY, YXZ, YZX, ZXY, ZYX.
    • Slot 1 has 3 choices; slot 2 has 2 left; slot 3 has 1 left — \(3\times2\times1\).
  • By convention, \(0! = 1\) — there’s exactly one way to arrange zero objects: do nothing.
  • Useful cancellation: \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!} = n\times(n-1)\times\cdots\times(n-k+1)\) — the tail end of the factorial cancels out.
    • E.g. \(\dfrac{5!}{(5-3)!} = 5\times4\times3\) — the trailing \(2\times1\) cancels between numerator and denominator.

Permutations and Combinations

  • Selecting \(k\) items from \(n\), order matters, with replacement: \(n^k\) possible outcomes.
  • Selecting \(k\) from \(n\), order matters, without replacement (a permutation): \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\).
  • Selecting \(k\) from \(n\), order doesn’t matter, without replacement (a combination): \(\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) — read “n choose k.”
↪ Running example: choosing an unordered comparison group of 3 countries from a shortlist of 10 is a combination, $\binom{10}{3}=120$; ranking a top-3 by growth from that same shortlist is a permutation, $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$.

Why the Permutation Formula Works

  • \(\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}\): choosing \(k\) from \(n\), order matters, no replacement.
  • \(n!\) counts every possible full ordering of all \(n\) objects.
  • We only care about the first \(k\) picks — the leftover \(n-k\) slots can be filled in any of \((n-k)!\) ways, and we don’t care which.
  • Dividing \(n!\) by \((n-k)!\) cancels out exactly that irrelevant tail, leaving only the first \(k\) picks: \(n\times(n-1)\times\cdots\times(n-k+1)\).

Why the Combination Formula Works

  • \(\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\): same setup as a permutation, but order no longer matters.
  • Start from the permutation count \(\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\) — but every unordered group of \(k\) got counted once for each of its \(k!\) orderings.
  • Example: the 3-country group \(\{X,Y,Z\}\) shows up as \(3!=6\) different permutations (XYZ, XZY, YXZ, YZX, ZXY, ZYX) — all the same group, over-counted 6 times.
  • Dividing by \(k!\) collapses each cluster of duplicates back down to one group — that’s the extra \(k!\) in the denominator.

Combinations, Worked Step by Step

  • How many ways to form an unordered research team of 3 from a shortlist of 5 countries?
  • \(\dbinom{5}{3} = \dfrac{5!}{3!(5-3)!}\)
  • \(= \dfrac{120}{6 \times 2} = \dfrac{120}{12} = 10\)
  • Sanity check via the cancellation trick: \(\dfrac{5!}{2!} = 5\times4\times3=60\) ordered picks, then \(\div\,3! = 6\) collapses those into \(10\) unique unordered teams.

Combinations, Listed Out

  • With 5 countries \(\{V,W,X,Y,Z\}\), here are all \(\binom{5}{3}=10\) possible research teams of 3, listed out:
  • VWX, VWY, VWZ, VXY, VXZ, VYZ
  • WXY, WXZ, WYZ
  • XYZ
  • Ten teams, no more, no fewer — the formula isn’t an approximation, it’s exactly this list’s size.

Splitting Into Multiple Groups

  • Combinations chain together when everyone gets split into more than two groups.
  • Suppose our shortlist of 20 countries needs to be split into research sub-teams: 4 for polling data, 6 for economic data, 5 for conflict data, and the remaining 5 for institutional data.
  • Choose each sub-team in turn, shrinking the pool each time: \[\binom{20}{4}\binom{16}{6}\binom{10}{5}\binom{5}{5}\]
  • By the fundamental rule of counting, multiply the stages together: \(4845 \times 8008 \times 252 \times 1 \approx 9.78\) billion ways to split all 20 countries into the four teams.

Quick Drill: Counting

  • From a shortlist of 8 countries, how many ways to choose an unordered pair for a paired comparison? \(\binom{8}{2} = 28\)
  • From that same shortlist, how many ways to pick an ordered “most, then second-most” democratic pair? \(8 \cdot 7 = 56\)
  • With 4 possible democracy-score categories and 3 possible growth categories, how many (democracy-score category, growth category) pairs are there? \(4 \times 3 = 12\), by the fundamental rule
  • From a shortlist of 6 countries, selecting 3 to visit in order, with repeat visits allowed: how many itineraries? \(6^3 = 216\)
  • Same shortlist of 6, selecting 3 in order, no repeats: how many itineraries? \(6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 = 120\)
  • Same shortlist of 6, selecting an unordered group of 3 to visit: how many groups? \(\binom{6}{3} = 20\)

At Least One Success

  • Common problem: given a known \(P(\text{success})\) on one attempt, what’s \(P(\text{at least one success})\) over several independent attempts?
  • Example: a game of chance has a 10% chance of success. If you play 5 times, what’s the probability of winning at least once?
  • Warm-up (not the answer, but intuitive): the probability of winning all 5 in a row is just \((0.1)^5 = 0.00001\) — multiply the single-trial probability by itself.
  • “At least once” is easier the other way: find the probability of losing every time, then take the complement.
  • \(P(\text{lose all 5}) = (1-0.1)^5 = (0.9)^5\), so \(P(\text{win at least once}) = 1-(0.9)^5 \approx 0.41\).
  • Play 10 times instead of 5: \(1-(0.9)^{10}\approx 0.65\).

Quick Drill: Counting + Bayes, Together

  • I’m teaching a MathCamp class of 5 students, and — purely out of curiosity — I noticed 2 of them are left-handed. About 10–12.5% of people worldwide are left-handed. There’s also a popular myth that artistically-inclined people are left-handed far more often — say, 25% of the time. Just from this one clue, what’s the probability my class leans more “artsy” than a typical group?
  • Step 1 — Counting: ways to choose which 2 of the 5 are left-handed: $ = $ \(10\) — you’ll meet the general formula this belongs to, the Binomial distribution, later today.
  • Step 2 — Likelihoods: \(a = P(2\text{ of }5\mid\text{Artsy}) = \binom{5}{2}(0.25)^2(0.75)^3 = 0.2637\)
    • \(g = P(2\text{ of }5\mid\text{Typical}) = \binom{5}{2}(0.125)^2(0.875)^3 = 0.1047\)
  • Step 3 — Bayes’ rule, equal priors cancel: \(P(\text{Artsy}\mid\text{evidence}) = \dfrac{a}{a+g} = \dfrac{0.2637}{0.3684} \approx 0.716\)
  • About a 72% chance my class leans artsy — a meaningful shift from the 50% prior, but \(n=5\) is small, so two data points can’t settle it.

Random Variables & Expectation

From events to numbers — and the algebra of what to expect.

One Flip: A Familiar Ratio

Number of Heads:

\[X = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{when } H \\ 0 & \text{when } T \end{cases}\]

  • \(\Omega=\{H,T\}\) — two equally likely outcomes.
  • \(P(\text{heads}) = P(X=1) = \dfrac12\).

Three Flips: Counting Again

Number of Heads:

\[X = \begin{cases} 3 & \text{when } HHH \\ 2 & \text{when } HHT,\ HTH,\ THH \\ 1 & \text{when } HTT,\ THT,\ TTH \\ 0 & \text{when } TTT \end{cases}\]

  • \(\Omega\) has \(2^3=8\) equally likely outcomes, sorted above by \(X\).
  • \(P(X=2) = \dfrac38\).

Picking a Country

Polyarchy Score:

\[X = \begin{cases} 0.83 & \text{when country} = A_1 \\ 0.61 & \text{when country} = A_2 \\ 0.34 & \text{when country} = A_3 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 0.72 & \text{when country} = A_{20} \end{cases}\]

  • \(\Omega\) = 20 countries, labeled \(A_1,\ldots,A_{20}\). “Democracy” = \(\{X>0.5\}\).
  • \(P(X>0.5) = \dfrac{13}{20} = 0.65\).

The Pattern Underneath

  • Look back at all three: each time, “favorable” meant counting how many outcomes crossed some numeric line — heads \(=1\); heads \(=2\); polyarchy \(>0.5\). Every event you just computed a probability for was secretly defined by a number, even when it was phrased as a yes/no question.
  • That’s the whole intuition for a random variable: a number attached to every outcome — heads count, or a country’s polyarchy score — that was quietly doing the work behind “favorable” the entire time, before it ever had a name.
  • And once that number exists on its own, not just buried inside one event’s definition, you can ask far more than the single question you started with: not just “is polyarchy \(>0.5\)?” but any threshold — \(P(\text{polyarchy}<0.3)\), \(P(0.2<\text{polyarchy}<0.6)\) — all from the same underlying number, no new setup required.

Back to the Coin: A Worked Average

  • Full breakdown: \(P(0)=\frac18\), \(P(1)=\frac38\), \(P(2)=\frac38\), \(P(3)=\frac18\).
  • What’s the average number of heads across all 8 equally likely outcomes of 3 flips? Weight each value by how often it happens: \(0(\tfrac18)+1(\tfrac38)+2(\tfrac38)+3(\tfrac18) = 1.5\) — lands between 1 and 2, pulled toward whichever value has more outcomes stacked on it.
  • That weighted-average move — multiply each value by its own probability, add them up — is exactly what “expectation” means. It’ll get its own notation, \(E[X]\), shortly.

Random Variables: The Formal Definition

  • Formally, a random variable is a function \(X:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}\).
    • Takes an outcome, returns a number.
  • The outcome itself is not the random variable.
    • \(\Omega\) holds outcomes like \(HHT\) — not numbers.
    • \(X\) is the mapping drawn above, landing each outcome on \(\{0,1,2,3\}\subset\mathbb{R}\).
  • Where does “random” come from? The mapping \(X\) itself is fixed — \(X(HHT)=2\), always.
    • Randomness means the outcome of the experiment isn’t determined in advance: there’s some probability the event will occur, not certainty.
Technically it has to be a measurable function — a technical condition that automatically holds for every example we'll use, so not something to worry about.

What Is a Distribution?

  • Knowing \(X\) is a function isn’t the whole picture — the natural next question is how likely is each value \(X\) can take?
  • A distribution answers exactly that: it specifies the probability of every value (or range of values) \(X\) can take. It’s what turns “\(X\) is a number” into “here’s how likely each number is.”
  • The coin example already has one, informally: \(X=0\) with probability \(\frac18\), \(X=1\) with probability \(\frac38\), and so on — that list of (value, probability) pairs is the distribution of \(X\).
  • How you write down a distribution splits along the same discrete/continuous line as \(X\) itself — covered in full for each case over the next several slides.

Discrete Random Variables: Definition

  • A discrete \(X\) is one where you could, in principle, list every value it can take — the coin’s \(X\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) is a short, literal list.
  • \(X\) is discrete if it can take only a finite, or countably infinite, number of distinct values.
  • Notation: capital \(X\) denotes the random variable itself (the function, before we know the outcome); lowercase \(x\) denotes one realized value. \(p(x)=P(X=x)\) reads “the probability \(X\) takes the specific value \(x\).”
↪ Running example: $X$ = "number of heads in 5 coin flips" is discrete, taking values in $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ — a longer list than 3 flips, but still a list.

Discrete Random Variables: The PMF

  • The distribution of a discrete \(X\) is called a probability mass function (PMF): \(p(x) = P(X=x)\), satisfying \(0\le p(x)\le 1\) and \(\sum_x p(x) = 1\).
  • The coin’s PMF: \(p(0)=\frac18\), \(p(1)=\frac38\), \(p(2)=\frac38\), \(p(3)=\frac18\).

Discrete Random Variables: Expectation

  • \(E[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x)\) — the same \(\Sigma\) from Day 1, weighting each value by its own probability instead of \(\frac1n\).
  • For the coin: \(0(\tfrac18)+1(\tfrac38)+2(\tfrac38)+3(\tfrac18) = 1.5\).
  • Works because there’s a finite list of \(x\)’s to sum over — that’s what “discrete” buys you.

Discrete Random Variables: The CDF

  • Instead of “\(X\) equals exactly this value,” ask “\(X\) is at most this value” — accumulate the PMF as you go.
  • \(F(x) = P(X\le x) = \sum_{i\le x} p(i)\).
  • Non-decreasing; \(\to0\) as \(x\to-\infty\); \(\to1\) as \(x\to\infty\).
Technically F is also right-continuous — a detail that matters more for the continuous case, so not something to dwell on here.

PMF Example: The Binomial Distribution

  • That shape has a name: the Binomial distribution — successes (“heads”) across \(n\) independent trials, each with success probability \(p\).
  • \(p(y) = \dbinom{n}{y}\,p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\). Check \(y=2\): \(\dbinom32(0.5)^2(0.5)^1 = \dfrac38\) — matches.
  • Mean: \(\mu = np = 3\times0.5 = 1.5\) — matches the average from a few slides back.

From Bars to a Curve

  • 5 flips: 6 bars. 10 flips: 11 bars. More trials, thinner and more numerous bars.
  • Polyarchy score doesn’t wait for “more trials” — it’s already a number on a continuum. No bars, just a curve: \(f(x)\).
  • Area under the curve is still probability — the shaded region above is \(P(X>0.5)\), same idea as summing bars, just with \(\int\) instead of \(\Sigma\).

Continuous Random Variables: Definition

  • \(X\) is continuous if there’s a nonnegative \(f(x)\) such that for any interval \(A\), \(P(X\in A) = \int_A f(x)\,dx\).
  • No \(p(x)=P(X=x)\) to list — for continuous \(X\), \(P(X=x)=0\) at every single point.
↪ Running example: $X$ = democracy score is continuous, $X:\Omega \to [0,1]$; $x=0.83$ is the realized value for one particular country — no countable list of possible values the way the coin had one.

Continuous Random Variables: The PDF

  • Probability density function (PDF): \(f(x)\ge 0\), \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\) — the continuous analog of \(\sum_x p(x)=1\).
  • \(P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) — area under the curve between \(a\) and \(b\).
  • \(E[X] = \int x\, f(x)\, dx\) — same “weight by probability, add up” idea, \(\int\) standing in for \(\Sigma\).
  • Not building fluency with \(\int\) yet — that’s Integration & Area Under the Curve, later today.

Continuous Random Variables: The CDF (Preview)

  • Same idea as the discrete CDF, accumulated with an integral instead of a sum: \(F(x) = P(X\le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(s)\,ds\).
  • Linked by the derivative: \(f(x) = \dfrac{dF(x)}{dx}\) — a preview of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, coming later today.
  • Preview only — no computation practice until Integration is covered.

\(Y=1\) \(Y=0\)
\(X=0\) 1/8 1/8
\(X=1\) 2/8 2/8
\(X=2\) 1/8 1/8

  • The joint PMF assigns a probability to every pair: \(p(x,y) = P(X=x, Y=y)\).
  • Check: \(\frac18+\frac18+\frac28+\frac28+\frac18+\frac18=1\) — all 8 outcomes accounted for.

The Marginal: Coin Flips

\(Y=1\) \(Y=0\) \(p_X\)
\(X=0\) 1/8 1/8 2/8
\(X=1\) 2/8 2/8 4/8
\(X=2\) 1/8 1/8 2/8
  • Forget \(Y\) — what’s \(X\)’s own distribution? Sum each row: \(p_X(0)=\frac28\), \(p_X(1)=\frac48\), \(p_X(2)=\frac28\).
  • That’s \(\frac14,\frac12,\frac14\) — exactly Binomial(2, 0.5), the number of heads in 2 flips. Makes sense: \(X\) only depends on flips 1–2, so summing out \(Y\) just hands back its own distribution untouched.

The Marginal

\(G=1\) \(G=0\) \(p_D\)
\(D=1\) 0.25 0.40 0.65
\(D=0\) 0.15 0.20 0.35
  • Same move as the coin table: forget \(G\) entirely — what’s \(D\)’s own distribution, on its own? That’s the marginal — and the name is literal: it’s the row total sitting in the margin of the table.
  • Get it by collapsing the joint table down to one variable: add across a whole row, ignoring which column it came from. \(p_D(x) = \sum_y p(x,y)\).
  • \(p_D(1) = 0.25+0.40=0.65\) — the \(D=1\) row, summed.
  • That’s exactly \(P(\text{Democracy})=13/20\) from Day 2. The marginal is the ordinary one-variable probability — this is just how to recover it once you start from the joint table instead of counting outcomes directly.

Expectation Algebra

Turning a random variable into a single number you can compute with.

From Weighted Sum to \(E[Y]\)

  • Recall the weighted-sum average from a few slides back — written with \(\Sigma\), that’s \(\sum_y y\,p(y)\).
  • Give that sum a name and a symbol: \(E[Y] = \sum_y y\,p(y)\). “\(E\)” is just notation for the weighted sum you already computed — nothing new mathematically.

What \(E[Y]\) Does and Doesn’t Mean

  • Not necessarily the value \(Y\) “should” take on any one trial — the average value of \(Y\) over many repetitions.
  • For the coin, \(E[X]=1.5\): no single flip ever lands on 1.5 heads, but 1.5 is what the average settles toward over many rounds of 3 flips.
  • For a function of \(Y\): \(E[g(Y)] = \sum_y g(y)\,p(y)\) — same weighted-sum idea, applied to \(g(Y)\) instead of \(Y\) itself.

Properties of Expectation

  • \(E[c] = c\) — a constant’s average is itself.
  • \(E[c\,g(Y)] = c\,E[g(Y)]\) — constants factor out.
  • Linear: \(E[g(Y_1)+\cdots+g(Y_n)] = E[g(Y_1)]+\cdots+E[g(Y_n)]\) — holds regardless of independence.
  • If \(X,Y\) independent: \(E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]\) — this one does need independence.

Conditional Expectation

  • \(E[Y\mid X=x] = \sum_y y\,p_{Y|X}(y|x)\)
    • Average \(Y\), but only over the slice where \(X=x\).
    • Built on the conditional distribution from a few slides back — same move, now averaged.
  • Used for prediction: know \(X\), don’t know \(Y\) yet — \(E[Y\mid X=x]\) is your best guess.

Conditional Expectation: Worked Example

  • \(E[G\mid D=1]\), step by step:
    • \(=1\times p_{G|D}(1\mid1) + 0\times p_{G|D}(0\mid1)\)
    • \(=1(0.385)+0(0.615)\)
    • \(=0.385\)
  • For a 0/1 indicator, conditional expectation is conditional probability.
    • Averaging a yes/no variable just hands back its own probability.
  • Continuous case, same shape: \(E[Y\mid X=x] = \int_y y\,f_{Y|X}(y|x)\,dy\) — preview only.

Conditional Expectation, Formally

  • Discrete: \(E[Y\mid X=x] = \sum_y y\,P(Y=y\mid X=x)\) — the definition already in use, just named.
  • Continuous: \(E[Y\mid X=x] = \int y\,f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy\) — same shape, sum replaced by integral.
  • Both are just \(E[Y]\)’s own definition, applied to the conditional distribution instead of the marginal one.

Properties of Conditional Expectation

  • Linear: \(E[aY+bZ\mid X=x] = aE[Y\mid X=x]+bE[Z\mid X=x]\) — same linearity as ordinary expectation.
  • Taking out what’s known: \(E[g(X)Y\mid X=x] = g(x)\,E[Y\mid X=x]\).
    • Once you’ve conditioned on \(X=x\), \(g(x)\) is just a number — pull it out of the average like any other constant.
  • \(\text{Var}(Y) = E[(Y-E[Y])^2] = E[Y^2]-(E[Y])^2\) — spread around the mean, and the computational shortcut.
  • \(SD(Y) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(Y)}\).
  • \(\text{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])] = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\) — do \(X\) and \(Y\) move together?
  • \(\text{Corr}(X,Y) = \dfrac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{SD(X)\,SD(Y)}\) — covariance, rescaled to \([-1,1]\).

Variance and Covariance: Properties

Reference list — not a drill, just what’s available when you need it later.

  • \(\text{Var}(c)=0\); \(\quad\text{Var}(cY)=c^2\text{Var}(Y)\).
  • \(\text{Cov}(Y,Y)=\text{Var}(Y)\); \(\quad\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\text{Cov}(Y,X)\).
  • \(\text{Cov}(aX,bY)=ab\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\); \(\quad\text{Cov}(X+a,Y)=\text{Cov}(X,Y)\).
  • \(\text{Cov}(X+Z,Y+W)=\text{Cov}(X,Y)+\text{Cov}(X,W)+\text{Cov}(Z,Y)+\text{Cov}(Z,W)\).
  • \(\text{Var}(X+Y) = \text{Var}(X)+\text{Var}(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\).

5 \(1\%\) 0.30 \(5\%\) 0.70
6 \(2\%\) 0.15 \(6\%\) 0.85

Question: Compute \(E[\text{Growth}\mid X=\text{bin}]\) for each of the 6 bins.

Solution: Bins 1–3

  • \(E[\text{Growth}\mid X=x] = (\text{Low value})\times P(\text{Low}) + (\text{High value})\times P(\text{High})\).
  • Bin 1: $(-2)(0.75) + (1)(0.25) = -1.5+0.25 = $ \(-1.25\%\)
  • Bin 2: $(-1)(0.65) + (2)(0.35) = -0.65+0.70 = $ \(0.05\%\)
  • Bin 3: $(0)(0.55) + (3)(0.45) = 0+1.35 = $ \(1.35\%\)

Solution: Bins 4–6

  • Bin 4: $(1)(0.45) + (4)(0.55) = 0.45+2.20 = $ \(2.65\%\)
  • Bin 5: $(1)(0.30) + (5)(0.70) = 0.30+3.50 = $ \(3.80\%\)
  • Bin 6: $(2)(0.15) + (6)(0.85) = 0.30+5.10 = $ \(5.40\%\)
  • All six together: \(-1.25\%,\ 0.05\%,\ 1.35\%,\ 2.65\%,\ 3.80\%,\ 5.40\%\)
    • Increasing across bins, even though every row used different growth values and probabilities — that sequence itself is the discretized CEF, the same object the sliding-window widget traced continuously.

Special Distributions

Reference only — named shapes you’ll meet again, not something to build fluency in today.

The Binomial Distribution

  • Counting successes across \(n\) independent trials, each with success probability \(p\) — the coin’s PMF, generalized.
  • \(p(y) = \dbinom{n}{y}p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\), for \(y=0,1,\ldots,n\).
  • Mean \(\mu=np\); variance \(\sigma^2=np(1-p)\).

The Poisson Distribution

  • Models counts of rare events over a fixed window — the discrete analog of “how many, not whether.”
  • \(P(Y=y) = \dfrac{\lambda^y}{y!}e^{-\lambda}\), for \(y=0,1,2,\ldots\)
  • Mean \(=\) variance \(=\lambda\) — one parameter does both jobs.

The Uniform Distribution

  • Every value in an interval \((\alpha,\beta)\) is equally likely — flat density, no peak.
  • \(f(y) = \dfrac{1}{\beta-\alpha}\), for \(\alpha\le y\le\beta\).
  • Mean \(=\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\); variance \(=\dfrac{(\beta-\alpha)^2}{12}\).

The Normal Distribution

  • The bell curve — symmetric, one peak, tails that never quite touch zero.
  • \(f(y) = \dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(y-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\).
  • Mean \(=\mu\); variance \(=\sigma^2\) — the two parameters are the shape.

Recap: The Toolkit

  • Combinatorics: counting without listing — the fundamental rule, permutations, combinations.
  • Random variables: turning outcomes into numbers, \(X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\) — discrete (PMF, CDF) and continuous (PDF).
  • Joint, marginal, conditional distributions: the coin (independent — conditioning changes nothing) vs. democracy (dependent — conditioning moves the distribution).
  • Expectation algebra: \(E[Y]=\sum_y y\,p(y)\), linearity, and the conditional version \(E[Y\mid X=x]\) — the seed of the CEF.
  • Variance and covariance: spread around the mean, and whether two variables move together.
  • Special distributions: Binomial, Poisson, Uniform, Normal — named shapes to recognize, not build fluency in yet.
