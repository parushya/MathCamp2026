Beyond Probability, RVs, and Expectation

Now we also want to know:

  • What’s the chance that something lies between particular values?
    • e.g. polyarchy score — does it exceed 0.5?
    • Sum up infinitely small pieces and integrate!
  • At particular values of any RV, how does the chance — the probability — change?
    • Differentiate the distribution / PDF to find out.

Today

  • First, what the tools are for — learn to read \(\int\), \(\lim\), and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\): integration as area under a curve, limits as the shrinking move underneath it, differentiation as change at a point.
  • Then, how to compute them — in the order the math builds: limits → derivatives → integrals.
    • Saving integration’s mechanics for last is what makes the antiderivative a one-line observation instead of a mystery.
  • The Join — deriving bivariate \(\hat\beta\) from a minimization criterion.

Refresher: PMF, PDF, Expectation

  • PMF — discrete \(X\): \(p(x) = P(X = x)\); each bar’s height is a probability.
    • \(p(x) \ge 0\) and \(\displaystyle\sum_x p(x) = 1\).
  • PDF — continuous \(X\): heights are densities; only areas are probabilities.
    • \(f(x) \ge 0\), \(\;\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1\), \(\;P(a \le X \le b) = \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).
  • Expectation — the probability-weighted average: the distribution’s balance point.
    • Discrete: \(E[X] = \displaystyle\sum_x x\,p(x)\).
    • Continuous: \(E[X] = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx\) — \(\int\) replaces \(\sum\), \(f(x)\,dx\) replaces \(p(x)\).

Integration & Area Under the Curve

Where probability lives for continuous variables: the area under a curve.

Why We Need It

  • Binomial(\(n=10\), \(p=0.5\)): 10 coin flips, \(k\) = number of heads.
    • PMF: \(P(X=k) = \binom{10}{k}(0.5)^{10}\) — one bar per possible outcome.
    • \(P(X=4) = \binom{10}{4}(0.5)^{10} = \dfrac{210}{1024} \approx 0.205\) — read straight off the bar.

The Same Toolkit, Concretely

Every PMF/CDF/\(E[X]\) we’ve used is a finite sum, \(\Sigma\), over the bars above.

  • PMF: \(\sum_{k=0}^{10} p(k) = 1\) — all eleven bars add to 1.
  • CDF: \(F(4) = \sum_{k=0}^{4} p(k) = \dfrac{1+10+45+120+210}{1024} \approx 0.377\).
  • \(E[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{10} k\,p(k) = 10(0.5) = 5\).

The Same Question, Continuously

  • No bars here — \(f(x)\) is a curve, and \(P(X=x)=0\) at any single point.
    • “What’s the chance \(X\) is between \(a\) and \(b\)?” is the shaded area under \(f(x)\).
  • There’s no list of bars left to add — we need a way to sum the area under a curve.
    • That’s what integration is for.

The Riemann Sum

  • Coarse strips: \(\sum_i f(x_i)\,\Delta x\) — visibly off around the curve.
  • Thinner strips fit the curve almost exactly — that gap keeps shrinking as \(\Delta x \to 0\).
    • That little arrow is hiding a promise. We’ll name it properly in the next section.

Density vs. Probability

  • Why the split? A discrete RV has countably many values — probability can sit on each one as a positive mass and still sum to \(1\).
    • A continuous RV has uncountably many values — if every one carried positive probability, they couldn’t possibly sum to \(1\); there are too many of them.
    • So probability gets smeared as a rate over the continuum instead of stacked as mass on each point — that rate is the density.

From Density to Probability

  • \(f(x)\) is a density, not a probability — it can even be \(>1\). Only an area under it is a probability.
    • Compare: a PMF bar height \(p(x)\) is the probability, directly.
  • To get an actual probability, multiply density by a width: \(P(x\le X\le x+dx) \approx f(x)\,dx\).
  • Shrink that width to a single point (\(dx\to 0\)) and the area vanishes — that’s why \(P(X=x)=0\) for any one value.

Notation for the Integral

  • \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) — the limit of that sum as the strips get infinitely thin.
  • Read \(\int\) as “sum” and \(dx\) as an infinitesimally thin strip width — the same job as \(\Sigma\) and \(\Delta x\), just in the limit.
  • \(a\) and \(b\): where the strips start and stop.

Anatomy of the Integral

  • \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) has the same three parts as \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=a}^{b}\):
    • \(f(x)\) is the integrand — what gets summed, same job as \(f(x_i)\) inside \(\sum_i f(x_i)\).
    • \(dx\) is the variable of integration — the infinitesimal step, same job as the index (or \(\Delta x\)) in \(\Sigma\).
    • \(a\) and \(b\) are the limits of integration — same job as the bounds on \(\Sigma\).

This Is Already Familiar

  • \(P(a\le X\le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) — area under the PDF between \(a\) and \(b\).
  • \(E[X] = \int x\,f(x)\,dx\) — same “weight by probability, add up” idea as \(\sum_x x\,p(x)\), with \(\int\) standing in for \(\Sigma\).
  • Day 3 used \(\int\) as a symbol; today it’s a Riemann-sum limit — a shrinking sum of rectangles, same as \(\Sigma\) but with the step size going to zero.

Quick Drill: Reading \(\int\) (1/3)

  • Is \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 f(x)\,dx\) a number or a function? What about \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx\)?
    • Answer: with bounds, a single number — the area from \(1\) to \(3\). Without bounds, a whole function — it gets a name (the antiderivative) when we solve integrals later today.
  • Given \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), state in words what it represents.
    • Answer: the area under \(f\) between \(a\) and \(b\) — and when \(f\) is a PDF, that area is \(P(a\le X\le b)\).

Quick Drill: Reading \(\int\) (2/3)

  • Discrete CDF: \(F(4)=\sum_{k=0}^{4} p(k)\). For a continuous \(X\), \(F(4)\) becomes…
    • (a) \(\sum_{x\le 4} f(x)\)
    • (b) \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{4} f(x)\,dx\)
    • (c) \(f(4)\)
    • Answer: (b) — \(\Sigma \to \int\), \(p(k) \to f(x)\,dx\). (a) can’t work: uncountably many points, each with probability \(0\). (c) is a density height, not a probability.
  • Discrete mean: \(E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\). The continuous version is…
    • (a) \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx\)
    • (b) \(\displaystyle\int x\,f(x)\,dx\)
    • (c) \(\displaystyle\int x\,dx\)
    • Answer: (b) — same “weight each value, add up” move. (a) is total probability, always \(1\). (c) forgets the distribution entirely.

Quick Drill: Reading \(\int\) (3/3)

  • Plot A — the shaded area is…
    • (a) \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)
    • (b) \(f(b)-f(a)\)
    • (c) \(\int_a^b x\,f(x)\,dx\)
    • Answer: (a) — the strip-sum between \(a\) and \(b\); for a PDF, \(P(a\le X\le b)\). (b) is two heights, not an area; (c) is expectation’s integrand.
  • Plot B — everything shaded from the far left up to \(c\) is…
    • (a) \(f(c)\)
    • (b) \(\int_{-\infty}^{c} f(x)\,dx\)
    • (c) \(\int_{c}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\)
    • Answer: (b) — all probability up to \(c\): the CDF \(F(c)=P(X\le c)\). (a) is a height (a density); (c) is the other tail, \(1-F(c)\).

Limits

Naming the move we’ve been leaning on all day: shrink a step to zero, see where things settle.

Why We Need It

  • We just shrank a width to zero and trusted the result to settle: rectangles → area. Day 3’s bars → curve was the same move, asserted without a name.
    • “Settles” is doing real work there — some things don’t settle at all.
  • Differentiation, coming up, is defined as a limit — we can’t even write it down without one.

From Bars to a Curve, Revisited

  • Day 3: 5 flips gave 6 bars, 10 flips gave 11 bars — more trials, thinner and more numerous bars, until there were no bars left, just a curve \(f(x)\).
  • That’s a limit: a sum of rectangle areas, \(\sum_i f(x_i)\,\Delta x\), as \(\Delta x \to 0\).

Getting Arbitrarily Close

  • A sequence is an ordered list of numbers: \(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots\) — here \(x_n = 1 + \frac{3}{n}\): \(\;4,\; 2.5,\; 2,\; 1.75,\; \dots\)
  • The terms never reach \(1\), but past any point they stay as close to \(1\) as you like — the sequence converges to \(1\).
  • \(\{i\} = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) never settles — it diverges.

Infinite Sums

  • A series is the sum of a sequence — the \(\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i\) we’ve used since Day 1, now with \(N \to \infty\).
  • \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^i} = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{8} + \dots = 2\) — infinitely many terms, finite total.
    • Every PMF fact like \(\sum_k p(k) = 1\) over countably many outcomes is this kind of convergent series.
  • \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} i = 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots\) blows up — divergent.

  • Same density you dragged on Day 3. Squeeze \(x\) toward \(c\) from below (red) and from above (green): the two heights \(f(x)\) get pinned as tightly around one number as you like — without ever needing to arrive at \(c\).
  • That number is what the next slide’s notation names: the limit of \(f\) at \(c\).

Limit Notation

  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = L\) — “as \(n\) grows without bound, \(x_n\) gets arbitrarily close to \(L\).”
    • Converges: a finite limit \(L\) exists. Diverges: no limit, or \(\pm\infty\).
  • Functions have limits too: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = L\) — as \(x\) steps closer and closer to \(c\), \(f(x)\) closes in on \(L\).
    • Now \(x\) can approach from below or from above: \(\lim_{x\to c^-}\) and \(\lim_{x\to c^+}\). The limit exists when both sides agree.
  • \(f\) doesn’t have to be defined at \(c\) for the limit to exist — only near it. That loophole is about to matter.

  • The next slide is nothing more than this picture, written down.

The Riemann-Sum Limit, Formally

  • \(\displaystyle \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\,\Delta x\) — the bars-to-curve picture, written down. The Riemann sum’s arrow, cashed in.
  • Every \(\Sigma\) from Days 1–2 has this same shape: index, terms, add up. The only new piece is \(\Delta x \to 0\).
  • Same move underwrites \(E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\) becoming \(E[X]=\int x\,f(x)\,dx\) for a continuous \(X\).

Quick Drill: Limits in Context (1/4)

  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \sum_i f(x_i)\,\Delta x\) is…
    • (a) \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)
    • (b) \(f'(x)\)
    • (c) it diverges — infinitely many terms
    • Answer: (a) — thinner strips, more of them, and the sum settles on the area. Infinitely many terms can still settle: that’s convergence.
  • Day 3’s binomial bars, with more and more flips and ever-thinner bars: the limiting object is…
    • (a) a CDF \(F(x)\)
    • (b) a PDF \(f(x)\)
    • (c) the expected value \(E[X]\)
    • Answer: (b) — the PMF’s bars melt into a density curve. Heights stop being probabilities; areas take over.

Quick Drill: Limits in Context (2/4)

  • A CDF \(F\), pushed all the way right: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} F(x)\) is…
    • (a) \(1\)
    • (b) \(\infty\)
    • (c) depends on the distribution
    • Answer: (a) — \(F(x)\) accumulates probability as \(x\) grows; in the limit it has accumulated all of it, and total probability is \(1\) for every distribution.
  • And pushed all the way left: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} F(x)\) is…
    • (a) \(-1\)
    • (b) \(0\)
    • (c) \(f(-\infty)\)
    • Answer: (b) — nothing accumulated yet. Every CDF carries these two limits built in: \(0\) on the far left, \(1\) on the far right.

Quick Drill: Limits in Context (3/4)

  • A discrete RV’s CDF is a staircase. At \(x = 1\), does \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} F(x)\) exist?
    • (a) yes — it’s \(0.75\)
    • (b) yes — it’s \(0.25\)
    • (c) no — from below \(F \to 0.25\), from above \(F \to 0.75\)
    • Answer: (c) — the one-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided limit doesn’t exist. And the jump is the PMF: its height, \(0.75 - 0.25 = 0.5\), is exactly \(P(X=1)\).

Quick Drill: Limits in Context (4/4)

  • A continuous RV’s CDF has no jumps — both one-sided limits agree everywhere. What does that say about \(P(X = x)\) at any exact value?
    • (a) \(P(X=x) = f(x)\)
    • (b) \(P(X=x) = 0\)
    • (c) can’t tell from the CDF
    • Answer: (b) — the jump height at \(x\) is exactly \(P(X=x)\); no jump means zero. Day 3’s “an exact value has probability \(0\)” is a statement about limits.
  • For any PMF \(p\): \(\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{N} p(k)\) is…
    • (a) \(1\)
    • (b) \(\infty\)
    • (c) \(0\)
    • Answer: (a) — “probabilities sum to \(1\)” over infinitely many outcomes is a convergent series, like \(\sum 1/2^i\) from the Infinite Sums slide.

Differentiation

The same shrinking-width limit as Integration, run the other direction.

Why We Need It

  • The opening slide’s second question is still open: at a particular value of an RV, how fast is the chance changing? A curve’s height alone won’t answer that — we need its slope.
  • Day 1 found \(\bar x\) minimizes \(\sum(x_i-c)^2\) by algebra alone. Most minimization criteria aren’t that friendly — we need a general tool for “where is this function flattest.”

Change We Can Already Measure

  • Across two years, change is a first difference: \(x_{t+1} - x_t\).
    • A country’s polyarchy score: \(0.42\) in 2010 \(\to\) \(0.45\) in 2011 — first difference \(+0.03\).
    • As a percentage change: \(\dfrac{x_{t+1}-x_t}{x_t} = \dfrac{0.03}{0.42} \approx +7\%\).
  • Day 3’s other running variable already is one: growth is the percentage first difference of GDP, year over year.
  • Both recipes need two snapshots — a year and the year after. Subtract, divide by the gap, done.
  • But ask “how fast was the score changing in 2010 itself?” and there’s only the 2010 number: nothing to subtract, and a zero-length gap to divide by.
    • The derivative is the tool that gets an honest answer out of exactly that situation — next slide.

Secant to Tangent

  • Let \(y(t)\) be the country’s polyarchy score over time. A secant joins two years, \(t\) and \(t+h\); its slope, \(\dfrac{y(t+h)-y(t)}{h}\), is the average change per year between them.
    • At \(h = 1\) it is the last slide’s first difference: \(y_{2011} - y_{2010}\) over a gap of one.
  • Shrink \(h \to 0\): the two years merge and the secant settles into the tangent — the score’s rate of change at the moment \(t\). The one-snapshot question, answered.
  • Same \(\Delta x \to 0\) move as the Riemann sum, aimed at a slope instead of an area.

Slope, in the Language of Probability

  • The CDF answers “how much?”: \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\) — the probability accumulated by the time you reach \(x\).
  • Its slope answers “how fast?”: how much new probability arrives per unit of \(x\), right at \(x\).
    • Concretely, Day 3’s density: \(F(0.50) = 0.313\) and \(F(0.51) = 0.328\) — so \(0.015\) of probability arrived in a window of width \(0.01\).
    • Rate: \(\dfrac{0.328 - 0.313}{0.01} \approx 1.5\) per unit of \(x\). Shrink the window and this rate settles — that’s a derivative.
  • Steep CDF → probability arriving fast → values near \(x\) common. Flat CDF → almost nothing arriving → values near \(x\) rare.
  • “Probability per unit of \(x\)” already has a name: the density. Next slide: the CDF’s slope is \(f(x)\), at every \(x\).

What a Derivative Is

  • \(f'(x) = \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0}\, \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\) — the limit of the secant slope.
  • Read it as the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\): the slope of the curve at the point \(x\).
  • \(f'\) is itself a function — a slope at every \(x\).

Quick Drill: Reading \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) (1/3)

  • Is \(f'(3)\) a number or a function? What about \(f'\)?
    • Answer: \(f'(3)\) is a single number — the slope of \(f\) right at \(x=3\). \(f'\) is a whole function: a slope at every \(x\). (Same split as \(\int_1^3 f\,dx\) vs. \(\int f\,dx\).)
  • \(f'(x)\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) — same thing or different things?
    • Answer: the same object, two notations: Lagrange’s \(f'(x)\) and Leibniz’s \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) (with \(y = f(x)\)). Both say: how much \(y\) moves per tiny nudge of \(x\) — a first difference with the gap shrunk to zero.
    • You’ll meet both in the wild — papers write \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\)-style Leibniz for clarity about what varies, \(f'\) for brevity.

Quick Drill: Reading \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) (2/3)

  • At which point is \(f'(x) = \dfrac{dy}{dx} = 0\)?
    • (a) A
    • (b) B
    • (c) C
    • Answer: (b) — the tangent at the peak is flat. Hold that thought: “find where the slope is zero” becomes our optimization tool this afternoon.
  • At which point is \(f'(x)\) negative?
    • (a) A
    • (b) B
    • (c) C
    • Answer: (c) — the curve is falling there, so the rate of change is negative. At A it’s rising: \(f'(x) > 0\).

Quick Drill: Reading \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) (3/3)

  • For a continuous RV, \(F'(x)\) is…
    • (a) \(f(x)\)
    • (b) \(P(X = x)\)
    • (c) the area under \(f\) up to \(x\)
    • Answer: (a) — the slider you just dragged: the CDF’s slope at \(x\) is the density. (b) is \(0\) for a continuous RV (last drill!); (c) is \(F(x)\) itself, not its slope.
  • Where the CDF is at its steepest, the PDF is…
    • (a) at its peak
    • (b) zero
    • (c) equal to \(1\)
    • Answer: (a) — steepest accumulation = highest density. And where the CDF has gone flat, \(f(x) = 0\): no probability arriving.

Trivia: Pebbles and a Stretched S

  • “Calculus” is Latin for “small pebble” — the counting stones of a Roman abacus. Medicine still uses the old sense: kidney stones are renal calculi.
    • Newton called his version “the method of fluxions”; Leibniz’s names — calculus differentialis and calculus integralis — are the ones that stuck.
  • The \(\int\) is a stretched S. Leibniz introduced it in a 1675 manuscript by elongating the long s (ſ) of Latin summa — “sum.”
    • The notation itself remembers what we built this morning: an integral is a sum of thin strips.
